Existing solution

SoTA: structured-light visuo-tactile sensors.

The state of the art is the structured-light visuo-tactile sensor, like the ones on this gripper. It gives a robot tactile information: how an object deforms and how much force is applied.

Visuo-tactile sensor

Tactile → visual: a camera reads touch.

It turns touch into an image: under structured light, a camera watches a soft gel deform as it is pressed, here by a screw. The colour changes reflect the applied force and the object's texture.

[Step 1] How do they work? Take Meta's Digit 360: a rigid surface over structured light and a camera. The camera reads a dent from how the lights shade it, which only works while the lights, the gel and the camera keep their geometry. So the base has to be rigid. [Step 2] (An illustration: the same press with the rigid base taken away.) Without it, the gel sags under a press, the optical geometry shifts, and the image no longer shows the dent. [Step 3] But that rigid base costs both things a robot needs. Compliance: a rigid surface cannot grip gently, like gripping with a knuckle instead of a fingertip. And ultra-sensitivity: on a rigid base, the gel barely deforms under an ultra-low force.

[Step 1] However, today's visuo-tactile sensors face a paradox: none is both sensitive and compliant. [Step 2] Most of them are rigid: sensitive, as the chart shows, but not compliant. [Step 3] Compliant ones, like Soft-Bubble, replace the rigid base with air and rely on tracking sparse markers on the membrane: soft, but far less sensitive. [Step 4] MoiréSkin fills this gap: it is compliant, and more sensitive than any of them. So how can one skin achieve both properties?

Simulation

[Step 1] The answer is to leverage a visual interference phenomenon, called the moiré pattern. It is generated when two gratings of fine parallel lines overlap at a small angle. [Step 2] We put them on the two faces of one soft film. Where they overlap, moiré fringes appear: each fringe is a row of points where the lines cross. (Scroll to bring the two gratings together.) [Step 3] Now touch the film. The dent squeezes the upper face and stretches the lower one: a tiny deformation, just one percent. Like nearly closed scissors, the two sets of lines barely move, but their crossing points slide a long way: the fringes there turn by about five degrees. So this film is a metasurface that amplifies touch. (Scroll to deform it more; hover to touch another place.) [Step 4] And here is our real sensor. A tiny yellow bean, lighter than one gram, is enough to deform these super-sensitive fringes.

We make the film with a technique borrowed from microfabrication. We print the fluorescent lower grating, then imprint the clear upper grating on the other side. Please see the details in our paper.

Two more designs make this work. First, the grating is fluorescent: blue LEDs excite it, so the fringes stay high-contrast in any light, even in darkness. Second, the camera can see through the skin, so the robot sees an object before touching it. It can line up before contact, for example by telling which side of a USB plug is up. And each cell costs under two dollars, with no cleanroom needed. (Hover a tile to show it on the sensor.)

[Step 1] Our first challenge: a skin needs both force sensitivity and spatial resolution, as our own fingertips show. A fingertip feels the lightest touch, and tells exactly where that touch is. [Step 2] The challenge is how to balance the two. A smaller angle between the two gratings gives more gain, so we can detect smaller forces, but it creates wider fringes with lower spatial resolution. (Scroll to change the angle; hover to press.) [Step 3] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place.) So we choose a balanced angle during fabrication. Here is the real sensor at this angle, as a finger presses it.

Sensing

How we detect force + position.

To read force and position, we feed the images before and after touch into a ResNet-50. It works in two stages: coarse first, then refined.

MoiréSkin reaches a normal force error of 3.78 millinewtons and a position error of 0.094 millimetres. That makes it the most sensitive visuo-tactile sensor reported to date: it senses the weight of a sticky note, and locates a touch to within a hair's width.

Actuation

Why do we need actuation?

Actuation enables dexterity for even a toy arm.

Toy-grade, low-precision arm

  1. 1Grasp the cup
  2. 2Water pours in

MoiréSkin is not only a sensor: it also actuates. A gripper already moves, so why actuate the skin? It adds a compliant layer right at the contact, which regulates the contact force and compensates for a low-precision arm, like this 50-dollar LEGO toy arm. (The label points at the skin on its gripper.) The arm grasps a cup. When water is poured in, the skin feels the weight and inflates to firm its grip. (Side by side, the same pour: on the left, an illustration of the cup falling out of the grip without tactile feedback; on the right, the real run with feedback.) Without this feedback, it would fall, as illustrated on the left. To control this inflation, the skin has to know its own internal pressure.

[Step 1] So our second challenge: reading the internal pressure for actuation. We read it from the fringes themselves, with no external pressure sensor: when the skin inflates, its centre is stretched, so the fringes there turn, and their rotation angle tells us the pressure. (Scroll to pump.) [Step 2] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place.) Here is the real sensor as its internal pressure changes. [Step 3] Our third challenge is drift. The chamber is sealed, so as the outside air pressure changes by 10 to 20 hectopascals a day, the fringes turn without any force. So how do we find the zero state automatically? [Step 4] Whenever the film bulges or sags, the centre and rim fringes turn in opposite directions. So we pump until they line up: the film is flat, and that is zero, found without any extra sensor. [Step 5] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place: the pump straightens the fringes.) Here is the real sensor during zero-state calibration.

We further show two case studies. First, it can feel softness the way we do: it inflates to press on an object. Being compliant, it holds objects as soft as tofu without crushing them. And because it is sensitive, it tells seven ultra-soft materials apart with up to 99.7 percent accuracy.
