Hi everyone, I'm Linzhen Zhu, a second-year PhD student in the Ambient Intelligence Lab at the University of Michigan. Today I'll introduce MoiréSkin, one of the most sensitive visuo-tactile sensors in the world.

Why it matters

Robots need both.

Dexterous robot manipulation requires two things: sensitivity, to handle fragile things, and compliance, to grip them gently. Without them, a fragile object ends up crushed, like this.

Visuo-tactile sensor

Tactile → visual: a camera reads touch.

So robots need a sense of touch. One way is a visuo-tactile sensor, just like the sensor on the gripper. Tactile sensing tells a robot how an object deforms and how much force is applied.

Our goal

MoiréSkin fills the gap.

[Step 1] Today's visuo-tactile sensors face a paradox: none is both sensitive and compliant. [Step 2] Rigid ones, like those built on a rigid base, [Step 3] or on an elastomer over a rigid base, [Step 4] or on a stiff dome, are sensitive but not compliant. [Step 5] Compliant ones, like Soft-Bubble, are soft but far less sensitive. [Step 6] So this corner is still empty. [Step 7] MoiréSkin fills this gap: it is compliant, and more sensitive than any of them. So how can one skin be both?

[Step 1] The answer is moiré. Take two fine gratings, 330 micrometres in pitch, too fine for a camera to see. (Scroll to bring them together.) [Step 2] Overlap them at a small angle, and broad moiré fringes appear; a tiny shift between them moves the fringes a lot. [Step 3] We put both gratings on one soft film, a metasurface, so one film generates moiré by itself.

Our key idea is to let moiré amplify touch. On the left, a touch dents the film by only micrometres, too little for a camera to see. Moiré amplifies it: on the right, the same dent moves the fringes by millimetres. (Hover over the membrane to press it.)

Fabrication

Two gratings → one soft film.

  1. Fluorescent lower grating · print it, cure at 65 °C, cast clear silicone over it, peel off
  2. Clear upper grating · spin-coat on the mold, imprint it on the other face
  3. One soft film · dry at 40 °C, a 330 μm grating on each face

No cleanroom · reusable molds

We make the film with a technique borrowed from microfabrication; the details are in our paper.

Two more designs make this work. First, the grating is fluorescent: blue LEDs excite it, so the fringes stay high-contrast in any light, even in darkness. Second, the skin is see-through, so the robot sees an object before touching it. It can line up before contact, for example by telling which side of a USB plug is up. And each cell costs under two dollars. (Hover a tile to show it on the sensor.)

[Step 1] Recall the two overlapped gratings. If one grating stretches or shrinks against the other, as when a touch dents the film, the fringes rotate one way or the other. The angle between them is our first design challenge: a trade-off. A smaller angle gives more gain, so we detect smaller forces, but wider fringes and lower spatial resolution; a larger angle does the opposite. So we choose a balanced angle of about 6 degrees, which puts the fringes 3 millimetres apart and amplifies the deformation about 9 times. (Scroll to change the angle; hover to press.) [Step 2] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place: a finger presses it.) [Step 3] Besides external forces, the skin also senses its own internal pressure, which we need for actuator control: it tells the pump how the skin has inflated. On the left, as the skin inflates or deflates, the centre and the rim stretch differently: when it inflates, the centre is stretched while the rim is compressed. On the right, the fringes turn further the more the film stretches, so their angle tells us the pressure; and the centre and rim turn in opposite directions. (Scroll to pump.) [Step 4] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place: it inflates and deflates.) [Step 5] Sensing pressure also brings our second challenge: zero drifts. The chamber is sealed, so as outside air pressure drifts by 10 to 20 hectopascals a day, the fringes turn without any force. [Step 6] Light does the same. Through the transparent film, any outside light shifts the apparent angle by a few degrees. (Move the mouse: it steers the lamp.) So where is zero? [Step 7] Recall that whenever the film bulges or sags, the centre and rim fringes turn in opposite directions. So we pump until they line up: the film is flat, and that is zero, found without any extra sensor. [Step 8] (The real sensor takes the simulation's place: the pump straightens the fringes, and that is zero.)

Sensing

How we detect force + position.

With the skin at its zero state, we can now sense touch. We feed the images before and after touch into a ResNet-50 with two readout heads. It estimates normal force and position in two stages: coarse first, then refined.

MoiréSkin reaches a normal force error of 3.78 millinewtons and a position error of 0.094 millimetres. That makes it the most sensitive visuo-tactile sensor reported to date.

Here we show two case studies built on MoiréSkin's ultra-sensitivity. With its unified sensing-actuation architecture, it can feel softness the way we do: it inflates to press on an object, like pressing it with a fingertip. Being compliant, it holds objects as soft as tofu without crushing them. And because it is sensitive, it tells seven ultra-soft materials apart with up to 99.7 percent accuracy.

MoiréSkin can also sense shear force. Holding a cup from the side, it weighs the water in real time as it is poured in. The error is 4.15 grams.

Sense + actuate

One pre-trained backbone, two new skills.

Both case studies reuse the same pre-trained backbone. Softness uses its features directly, with no fine-tuning, and shear force only fine-tunes it and adds a new head.
