Hi everyone, I'm Linzhen Zhu from the University of Michigan. In this talk, I'll introduce MoiréSkin: an ultra-sensitive visuo-tactile skin that is also a soft pneumatic actuator. This is joint work with Liuxi Xing, Runqiu Wang and Ke Sun.

Robot touch

A camera watches a soft gel.

Delicate grasping needs skin that feels tiny forces and adjusts its softness. Most visuo-tactile sensors, like this one on a gripper, use structured light and a camera to image a soft gel as it is pressed.

A 0.67-gram test

Can robot skin feel a peanut?

DIGITno visible change

MoiréSkinfringes bend

But for a stable image, their gel is stiff and opaque. Here is a 0.67-gram peanut. On DIGIT, a widely used visuo-tactile sensor, the image barely changes. On MoiréSkin, the fringes clearly bend where the peanut rests. So: can robot skin feel a peanut, and still stay soft?

Visuo-tactile sensors today

Sensitive, or soft?

This is not just DIGIT. Here are the visuo-tactile sensors we compare against, with their reported force error, roughly ordered from rigid to soft. Rigid designs like GelSlim and GelSight give high resolution, but their stiff elastomers saturate and cannot comply with irregular contact. Even MoiréTac, which also uses moiré, sits on a rigid substrate. Soft designs like Soft-Bubble comply, but stay around sub-newton precision. Their opaque gels hide the object, and almost none of them can actuate. MoiréSkin aims for the top-right corner: sensitive and soft, and it also actuates and sees through.

Our insight

Our key idea is moiré. Two fine gratings, overlapped, form broad moiré fringes. On the left, the film from the side: a touch dents it by only micrometres, far too little for a camera to see. But a tiny shift between the two gratings moves the fringes a lot, by millimetres, easy for any camera to see. The lens shows the real lines of both gratings. (Hover over the membrane to press it.)

A few small designs make this work. First, the lower grating is fluorescent: blue LEDs make it glow, so the fringes stay high-contrast in any ambient light. Second, the skin stays see-through: light from the object passes through the clear cover, which only filters out red light, to the camera. Here, a phone screen, a USB plug and a power adapter seen through it. And each cell costs under two dollars in materials, with no cleanroom; the whole system is about fifty dollars. [Step 1] So how does it work? One scene for the whole story: on the right, the membrane as our camera sees it; on the left, a cross-section of the film. Two fine gratings, an upper one and a fluorescent lower one, 330 micrometres apart: too fine for the camera to track. (Scroll to bring them together.) [Step 2] They sit on the two faces of one film, overlapped at a small angle, and broad moiré fringes appear, about ten times larger than the lines. [Step 3] The angle sets the gain, and this is our first design challenge. A smaller angle gives more gain: a tiny deformation turns the fringes more, so sensitivity rises. But the fringes also get wider, and spatial resolution drops. The two curves cross at 6.31 degrees: ten fringes across 30 millimetres, with about nine-times gain. Now a touch: it dents the film, the upper face is compressed and the lower face stretched, and the fringes distort locally around the contact. In the corner, the real sensor under a finger. (Scroll to change the angle; hover to press.) [Step 4] The same skin also feels its own pressure. Inflation rotates the entire fringe pattern, while touch distorts it locally, so one camera tells internal inflation from external touch, and the fringe angle gives us the chamber pressure, our actuation state. In the corner, the real sensor inflating. (Scroll to pump.) [Step 5] Which brings our second challenge: zero drifts. The chamber is sealed, but the weather is not. Outside air pressure drifts by 10 to 20 hectopascals a day, comparable to our whole actuation range, and the fringes turn although nobody touched or pumped anything. [Step 6] Light does the same. Because the cover is transparent, a lamp outside the skin shifts the apparent fringe angle by a few degrees. (Move the mouse: it steers the lamp.) So where is zero? [Step 7] We use the clamp. The film meets the frame flat, so near the rim its bend reverses: when the film sags or bulges, the centre and rim fringes turn opposite ways, as the two dials show. Only when the film is flat are they parallel. A PID loop pumps against the bend until they line up, within two seconds. That is the zero state, found from a single image, without any pressure sensor.

Sensing

Two images → force + position.

Now sensing. Images before and after touch estimate contact position and normal force. We feed both images as two channels to a ResNet-50 with an attention module. Stage one predicts coarse force and position; stage two refines each with the other, because stiffness varies across the inflated skin and inflation shifts positions. In our ablation, the pre-contact image cuts the tiny-force error by 45 percent.

In the tiny-force tests, a 0.72-millimetre probe up to 0.4 newtons at the zero state, on unseen positions, MoiréSkin reaches 3.78 millinewtons of force error and 0.094 millimetres of position error. Under random inflation up to 4 newtons, 94.9 percent of force errors stay below 0.1 newton. On the right, the same sensors as before: from GelSight at 1.9 newtons to ViTacTip at 0.01. MoiréSkin reaches 0.0038, on a skin that also actuates. Inference runs in under a millisecond on a GPU and 22 milliseconds on a laptop.

Sense + actuate

An active probe.

0%10-shot · 7 materials
92.7%5-shot
8.7–21.7kPaas soft as tofu
Now sensing and actuation together. MoiréSkin can act as an active probe. A soft sphere sits between the skin and a rigid support; the skin first just holds it, then inflates to press against it. How the fringes change between the two states tells its stiffness. We distinguish seven ultra-soft materials, as soft as tofu, from 8.7 to 21.7 kilopascals: 92.7 percent with 5 shots and 99.7 percent with 10, using our tactile backbone without fine-tuning and a simple LDA classifier.

Weighing a filling cup.

And a changing load. The skin inflates to clamp a cup against a jaw, then holds while water is poured in, up to about 300 millilitres. From the fringes, MoiréSkin tracks the liquid mass with 4.15 grams mean error and 4.75 grams RMSE on an unseen session.

Application

A toy arm, a precise touch.

Toy-grade, low-precision arm

  1. 1Grasp the cup
  2. 2Water pours in
  3. 3Feels the weight, inflates
  4. 4Holds steady, sets it down
Now on robots. In this demo, MoiréSkin enables a toy LEGO robotic arm to manipulate objects with professional-level precision. The arm grasps a cup; water pours in; the skin feels the added weight and inflates to firm its grip; then it holds steady and sets the cup down. Its ultrasensitive tactile feedback helps compensate for errors in the arm's control.

To summarize. MoiréSkin is ultra-sensitive: 3.78 millinewtons. It is soft and actuated, and it feels its own pressure. It finds its own zero by vision, in under two seconds. And it stays see-through.
